### Client

`App.tsx` holds all top-level state and persists it in the **URL query string** (seeded from `window.location.search` on load, synced via `history.replaceState`) — so a reload or shared link restores the same view. There is no `localStorage`. Params: `preset` **or** `start`/`end` (never both), expanded **player keys**, open details player (also a key), `view=feed`, `kind=pitcher`, `sim=1`, `help=1` (the how-to page — in the URL like every other view, so it survives a reload and can be handed to someone directly). **A preset is a rule, not a range** — while one is active the URL carries only its label and the dates are re-derived from `datePresets()` on load, so a link (or a reloaded tab) saved under "Today" opens on the *new* today after the date rolls over. Only a custom range from the picker writes `start`/`end`. An unrecognised `preset=` label falls back to whatever `start`/`end` say; legacy links that carry both follow the preset. `readKeys()` also accepts bare ids in `expanded`/`player` — links from before two-way support, read as batters.

### Two lists: the roster and the watchlist

The app holds **two** per-user lists of players, and they mean different things:

- **Roster** — what the Roster and Feed views report on. The list built with the header search and ordered on the edit screen, or, in fantasy mode, the user's ESPN team. `PlayerDetails` says `On roster` beside its accent baseball, and `RemoveButton` takes a player off it.

  **Over a range that list is the roster as it *stood* on those days**, which is the server's doing and reaches the client as an ordinary report: `/api/report` returns everyone who was on the roster on any day of the range, each carrying only the games of the days he was actually held (see **The roster is a range of rosters** in **Roster, watchlist, users and auth**). So a man added this morning has no row over "Yesterday", and a man dropped this morning still has his week. Nothing in the views had to be told — the summary table's rows, its `Total` and every feed item sum `report.games`, which is the same property the fantasy `Starters` projection leans on.

  **What did not change is "is he on my roster *now*"**, which is a different question and is asked by four controls: the research board's `My Roster` button and its accent baseball, `PlayerDetails`' badge and its Remove, and `PlayerAdder`'s dedupe. All of them read `rosterKeys` or `roster` — the live list off `/api/watchlist` — and all of them still mean today, so a dropped player is findable and addable again and his page offers `Add to roster` rather than a badge. **The one that had to move is the reorder screen**: `editPlayers` read raw `reports` and would have offered to drag and delete a man who is not on the list it edits, so it filters on `rosterKeys` first. That is the honest version of the rule the screen already had — it is deliberately upstream of hide-injured and of `Starters` because dropping a player is what it is *for*, and a row for somebody already dropped is the one thing it cannot do anything about.
- **Watchlist** — a set of player keys followed on the **research board**, independent of the roster. A free agent you are thinking about picking up is on your watchlist and not on your roster, which is the case the app previously had nowhere to express: the only list it had was the one every view reported on, so "keep half an eye on this man" and "report on him every day" were the same act.

The words are now used one way each, everywhere. See **Roster, watchlist, users and auth** for what the server calls them (and for the two places the old word survives on purpose: the `WATCHLIST_TABLE` env var and the `/api/watchlist` routes, which are the *roster's*).

### Player keys

A roster entry is identified by **`${kind}-${id}`** (`playerKey()` in both `types.ts` files), not by the MLB id: a **two-way player is two entries**, watched as a hitter, as a pitcher, or both. `getSeasonPlayers` emits a row per kind for primary-position code `Y`, so the roster search offers Ohtani twice. Everything downstream keys on that: `store.ts` (add dedupes on kind+id; `removeRosterPlayer(id, kind)` — omitting `kind` removes every entry for the id, which is what a pre-two-way client means), `PlayerAdder`'s watched-set and `detailsKey`. (Three more have gone: `expandedKeys` and the `id="player-<key>"` the summary table's names used to scroll to, retired when one player's day became the player page's own tab and the jump became a plain `setDetailsKey`; and the feed's own `feedOpenKeys`, retired when every openable shape in the feed became a **popup** rather than an accordion and its open state stopped being anything App had to name — see **Details are popups, not accordions** below.) `PUT /api/watchlist/order` takes **`keys`**, usually just one kind's, and `reorderRoster` splices them back into the slots that kind already occupied so the other kind never moves. **The watchlist is nothing but those keys** — `PUT /api/watch` takes one and a boolean — which is what makes a two-way player watchable as a pitcher and not as a batter, exactly as he is rosterable as one and not the other.

**Three views, one row of tabs: Roster · Feed · Research.** Roster is the summary table over the date range, Feed the same players and days read as a stream, Research the whole league over the season.

It was two tiers once, and the thing that collapsed it is worth keeping because it is the same observation twice. The top row was the real division — the roster over a range against the league over a season — and under it sat Roster's own **Summary / Games / Feed**, three readings of one set of players over one set of days. But Games and Feed were not two readings. Games was a card per player over the range; the feed was a stream over the same range; what differed was the **sort order**, roster against clock. Sorting is not a page. So Games became a grouping *on* the feed, and Roster was left holding a single reading with a whole tier of chrome to select it.

**That grouping has since gone one step further, and off this page altogether.** A card per player is a page about a **player**, and this app already had one that opens on anybody from every row of every view: `PlayerDetails`. So the reading moved onto it as the **Overview** tab (below), the toggle went with it, and a player's name anywhere now opens the same page his headshot does. What that bought is worth naming, because it is more than tidiness: the grouped feed could only ever draw the players on your roster, where the page it moved to opens on **strangers** — a research-board row is exactly the reader who most wants to know what a man did today, and had no way to ask.

What remains is three pages that really are three questions, and they read as siblings because they are: Summary is as different from Feed as either is from Research. **Feed sits in the middle**, between the roster it reports on and the league it doesn't.

**`view` is one piece of state again** (`'summary' | 'feed' | 'research'`), the `section`/`rosterTab` pair and the `setView` shim over them having existed only to hold the two tiers apart. `summary` is still the default and still omitted from the URL — renaming the param to match the `Roster` label would cost every link in the wild and buy a word.

**Three dead names are still read, and all of them mean the feed.** `view=games` was the card-per-player page and `view=players` its own older name; both became the feed's grouping, and the grouping is now the player page's Overview tab. The **feed** is where they still land, and that is the right answer rather than a lazy one: it is the same players over the same days, which is the closest thing to what those links asked for, where the page they *became* needs a player named and a bare `view=games` names none. **`group=player` and `expanded=` are read only in the sense that they are ignored** — the first URL sync drops both, the cards they named existing nowhere now. That is the courtesy `readKeys` extends to pre-two-way ids, and the safe direction for an old link to be read in: it opens on something, rather than on a default that says nothing about what was asked for.

The Roster and Feed pills only render once something is on the roster (`showRosterViews`); **Research needs no roster and is always there** — with nothing rostered the bar is that lone pill, which is the one tab a new user can actually use.

**Where the rows break is the window's business, not the markup's.** Every group in `.view-bar-tabs` is `flex: none`, so the wrapping row fits as many *whole* groups per line as the width allows and breaks between two rather than inside one. The roster tabs took a forced row of their own for a while (`flex: 1 1 100%`, which reads as "its own line" in a wrapping bar) and it was the wrong instinct twice over: a fixed break costs a desktop a line it has the room for, and it saves a phone none. The order is the order the questions come in — **which page, which kind, which reading, which days** — which is why the calendar sits at the end of the same row rather than anywhere else. Whose list it is used to be a fifth item here and is now the fantasy button's own lit state, up in the header.

**The app spans the window.** `.app` was a 1180px column centred in it, and on anything wider than a laptop that spent the difference on two empty margins — on the very views whose content is a wide table, which is where the room would have gone. The cap is gone and the gutters are all that is left of it (`padding: 14px var(--app-gutter) 80px`), so the page is the window less 22px a side. The pinned chrome follows for free: its negative side margins are those same 22px, so it still bleeds to the app's own gutters, and `--chrome-h` is measured at runtime rather than declared, so nothing that clears it had to be told the page got wider. Checked at 390, 1180 and 1920 on the summary, research, games and feed views: no horizontal overflow of the page body at any of them, and the bar spans the viewport exactly.

**And the three wide tables give even those gutters back** (`--table-bleed`). Dropping the cap was meant to put the summary table, the research board and the game log edge to edge and didn't quite: they still sat inside `.app`'s 22px a side, which is 44px of page they have no use for. On the research board, which overflows a 1920px screen with 25 of its 39 columns showing, that is a stat column pushed off the right edge; on a 390px phone it is 44 of the 346 the gutters leave. The pinned chrome had already solved this for itself — a negative side margin exactly the size of the padding it sits in — so the tables do the same, and the cards, the reading columns, the count line, the badge rows and the empty states keep their gutters while only the tables give them up.

**The bleed is declared by the container, not by the table**, because the number is that container's own padding and there are three of them: `.app` at 22px, the player-page overlay the game log lives inside at 16, and the full-page box all three can take at 12. One rule reading `--table-bleed` beats three rules carrying three numbers, since a table can then never bleed by more or less than the box it is in has to give — and the full-page mode falls out of it for free: the pane spans the viewport while `.expanded-chrome` and the board's count line keep their 12px. The sides of the box go with the margin (`border-left`/`right: none`, `border-radius: 0`): a 12px corner and a hairline sitting on the very edge of the screen read as a card that has been clipped, where without them it reads as the band it now is, the top and bottom rules still closing it. (The summary view later gave its *vertical* margins back on the same argument and dropped the top rule with them, the chrome's own hairline being directly above it — see its passage below.)

**Nothing about the sticky columns moves**, which is the half worth checking rather than assuming, since a bleed changes where the scrollport sits: `left: 0` and the research board's own name-column offset are measured from the scrollport's own padding edge, and the scrollport and its contents shift together. Measured at 320 / 390 / 640 / 820 / 1200 / 1920, scrolled to the far right and down on each — no horizontal overflow of the page body at any width; the headshot column pins at 0 and the research board's name column at exactly **68** from 820px up, which is the 13 + 42 + 13 its offset derives (63 while the circle was 37 — see **The tables breathe** below, which is also where that constant stopped being one); `--research-pin-left` still measures the pinned block rather than a position (264px at 1200 on a re-checked board, 52–62px below the breakpoint where the headshot is all that pins); the header row and the game log's date column pin at 1px, which is the border; and the total row is still 1px off the bottom of its box. The tables gained the full 44px at every width: the summary table now runs 0→1920 in a 1920 window and 0→390 on a phone, the game log 0→1920 inside an overlay whose head and `Load more` keep their 16px, and an expanded board 0→1920 under a badge row that keeps its 12.

**The tables breathe, and every number in this section was re-measured when they did.** The three wide tables were tight — a 51px row built from a 37px headshot and 10px of cell padding, read down a column of thirty names — and a stat table read that way wants air more than it wants one more row on screen. The row is now **58px**, off a **42px** headshot and 12px of padding, and the gutters between columns grew at the end where there was room. Both halves of the trade are measured rather than asserted, and the constraints the tables had already been tuned against were re-checked one by one.

**The vertical half is spent where it is cheap and the horizontal half only where there is slack.** A row's height is paid in scrolling; a column's width is paid in a stat pushed off the right edge of a phone, which is the whole reason the gutters are `clamp()`ed at all. So the vertical padding goes **10 → 12px** on the summary table and the research board alike (11 → 13 on the game log, and 8 → 10 on the board's own sort-button headers, which stay two under the body's because a header is one short line where a body row is a headshot), while the gutter clamp grows only on the two tables that had room for it: `clamp(5px, 1.6vw, 22px)` → **`clamp(5px, 1.9vw, 28px)`** on the summary table and the game log, with the **floor untouched**, so the narrowest screens the app is checked at pay the least of all. Measured on the real tables: the summary gutter goes 6.24 → 7.22px at 390 and 22 → 28 at 1920, the table itself **683 → 702px at 390** and 752 → 790 at 640, and stays exactly 1200 and 1920 at those widths — the numeric columns' `width: auto` absorbs the surplus, so a table that already fits spends the gutter out of its own slack rather than out of the page. The game log goes **736 → 772px at 390** and 40.6 → 44.6px a row.

**The research board keeps its 13px gutter, which is the exemption above doing exactly the job it was written for.** At 27 columns on a checked board a pixel a side is 54px of extra scroll, and the summary table's new clamp would have been 320 of them on a table already 1,881px wide in a 1200px window — two more stat columns pushed past the right edge to buy air the reader then has to swipe through. So that board takes the vertical half of the change and none of the horizontal, and its table grows by **5px at every width**, which is the headshot and nothing else.

**The headshot's five pixels come out of its own cell, not out of the row.** The image cell trades vertical padding for image — **6 + 46 + 6 = 58**, the 46 being the 42px circle plus the ~4px of descender the cell's own strut hangs under an inline image — against the **12 + 31 + 12 = 55** the research board's identity block asks for and the **12 + 30 + 12 = 54** the summary table's name cell does. So the circle is still the tallest thing in a row, exactly as it was at 5 + 37 + 5 = 51, and both boards measure **58.00px a row** (51.00 and 52.08 before) with the identity block unchanged at 31.08 and **three pixels of slack it did not have before**. The 6 also keeps the live-role ring — a 2px `box-shadow` outside a 1px border — clear of the row border with a pixel to spare.

**The cost is one row a screen, counted rather than estimated.** Rows entirely inside the pane in an 800px window, before → after on the research board: **7 → 6 at 320, 8 → 7 at 390, 10 → 9 at 640, 10 → 9 at 1200, 11 → 10 at 1920**. That is the price of six pixels a row (52 → 58 there, 51 → 58 on the summary table) and it is worth naming plainly, because it is the same currency the identity block was tuned in when `Tm` and `Pos` came off the grid.

**The board's `left: 63px` stopped being a number.** That offset *is* the headshot cell — the photo between two of the board's gutters — and it was written out as `63px` (13 + 37 + 13), which is the kind of constant that is right until somebody grows the circle: 42 makes it 68, and a stale 63 pins the name column five pixels *under* the headshot it exists to clear. It is now derived from the two things it is made of, `--row-photo` on `:root` and `--research-gutter` on `.research-table` — the same variable the cells are actually padded with, so the offset and the padding cannot drift — and the sorted column's pre-measurement fallback takes the same calc. Checked either side of the breakpoint: the gutter reaches its 13px cap at ~813px so the calc is a flat 68 everywhere the media query applies (819 → gutter 13, name not sticky, sorted column pinned at 68 = the headshot cell alone; 820, 821 and 900 → `left` computes `68px`, the name lands at exactly 68, and the sorted column at 264 = 68 + a 196px name). **820 is still the right breakpoint** and needed no move: it was picked for where the gutters reach 13px, which this change does not touch.

**The marks on the circle grew with it and were re-measured on it.** The lineup pip is **15 → 17px** (font 9 → 10, the two-glyph role pill 8 → 9) and keeps its `-2px` corner offset, which is arithmetic rather than laziness: a 15px pip at −2 on a 37px circle has its centre 15.8px from the photo's centre against a rim at 18.5, and a 17px pip at −2 on a 42px circle sits 17.9 against a rim at 21 — 2.7px inside the rim against 3.1, the same mark in the same place a fifth of a pixel apart. The status pill is **11 → 12px** tall at font 9, and because `bottom: -5px` fixes its *bottom* edge the extra pixel is spent upward: it still hangs 5px below the circle and now clears both the row and the sticky total row under the last row by **5px** (4 before — the image cell's extra padding, not the badge). `IL60`, the widest code the app can print, measures **32.3px inside the 42px circle with 4.8px clear of either rim**, where it was 28.1 inside 37 clearing 4.5 — so it gained air rather than losing it. And the `text-box: trim-both` centring holds at the new sizes, measured with a canvas against the rendered baseline exactly as it was when it was written: the digit pip is **0.01px** off dead centre, `SP` and the status code **0.00**.

**The player page's 64px portrait deliberately did not grow.** It is a page header rather than a table row — sized to the head it sits in and to the reading column beside it — where the row circle is a thing scanned down a column of thirty, which is the one that was too small. Growing both would only have moved the ratio and taken 5px off the top of a view whose head is already 23% of a phone. Its own two marks are sized to *it* rather than derived from the row's, so nothing about them moved: measured after the change, identical to before at every figure (24px pip, 16px pill, `IL60` at 38.7px with 12.6px clear of the rim, centring 0.01 and 0.00).

**Everything else the tables had already been measured against was re-checked and is unchanged.** At 320 / 390 / 640 / 1200 / 1920, scrolled to the far right and the bottom on each: **no horizontal overflow of the page body at any width**; `--table-bleed` still −22px with the pane at 0 from both edges; the summary view's vertical margins still **0 above and 0 below** with no top border on the pane; the sticky header row still flush at the top of its scrollport and the pinned total row still 1px off the bottom; the headshot column still pinned at 0 on both boards and the game log's date column at 0 with its header row at 1px, which is the border.

**What still carries a cap carries it deliberately.** Every `max-width` in the stylesheet was read again when the app's own came off, and each answers one of two questions. What is **read** stays a reading column and stays centred: the tutorial's prose (760px, its paragraphs at 62–64ch), the order editor and the edit screen's head (620px), the auth card, the settings and fantasy popovers, the search results menu (520px — eight names across a 1900px page would read as a page rather than as a dropdown), and the player page's own cards, where a percentile row is a label, a bar and a number, and spreading those over 1900px puts the label at one end of the room and the number at the other. What is **scanned** takes the window: the three wide tables — the game log included, which took two goes to get there. It went 1000px to **1400** first, on the argument that its fourteen columns fit at 1400 with no sideways scroll at all; but "fits" was never the question a cap answers. At 1400 in a 1920 window the log sat in the middle of the overlay with a margin either side and a dead gap in the opponent column, which is a table declining the room it was given rather than a reading column defending itself from it. It now has **no cap** and takes the window like the other two, while the 860px tabs beside it are cards and keep their column.

**The feed keeps a cap of its own**, `--card-column` (800px) — shared with the Games view's card column until that view folded into it. A list of cards is read down rather than across, and the number is set by what a card actually holds rather than by what the window has: the header is a name at one end and a line of stats at the other, so every pixel of width past that pushes the two apart and leaves the middle empty. It took two goes to get there. `none` first, on the argument that "full width like a phone" should mean every view; then 1600px, which is **no cap at all on the machine anyone uses** — a laptop being 1440 or 1512 wide and under it either way, so the cards went on filling the window while the token read as though it were doing something; then 1136, which is what `.app`'s old 1180px cap left inside its 44px of gutters and so was merely the width they had always been. A cap has to be narrower than the screen it is meant to hold, and this one narrower again than that. **800 rather than the 757 a strict third would give**, and the 43px is measured: at 760 the longest name on a real roster ("Pete Crow-Armstrong" with its position chip) wraps and takes the card header from 88px to 100, where at 800 nothing on the list wraps at all — not proof, since a longer name exists, but the width where the cost starts. It clears `.player-card`'s own container queries too (the phone layout under 480px, an intermediate one from 481 to 700), so an expanded card keeps its full pitch table and strike-zone plot. The feed takes the same number rather than keeping its old 860, its items being those same cards — a feed a different width from the view beside it reads as a different page rather than as another way of reading the same days. One thing had to be capped separately: the feed's inline clip (`.pa-video-inline`) is a 16:9 frame at `width: 100%` under a 360px height cap, which at the full width of a page was a black letterbox band with a postage stamp of baseball in the middle of it. `max-width: 640px` is that height cap read back through the ratio, so the frame is the size the clip actually plays at and sits at the left edge of its item like everything else in it.

**The chrome is pinned to the top of the window** (`.app-chrome`, `position: sticky`). The header, the search bar and the view bar were three siblings and are now one box, because they are one thing: each says *where you are and what you are looking at*, which is the last thing that should scroll away under a page being read. Which kind, which days and which view are what the numbers below **are**, and on the feed — where a roster's day runs to hundreds of items — they were an inch of page you had to go back up for. The negative side margins (and the matching padding) let it bleed to the app's own gutters, and the negative top one stops a sliver of page showing above it through `.app`'s 14px of top padding. `z-index: 41` puts it over everything in the page's flow (the float button at 20, the date popover at 30) and under the full-page table mode (45) and the overlays (50 and 60), which cover it as they cover everything else; its own children keep their z-indexes inside the stacking context that opens, so the settings popover and the search results still hang over the page below.

**On the research board the tab row holds its whole control set** (`.research-chrome`): the include buttons, the window tabs, the position row, the four disclosure buttons, whichever of their panels is open and the row of filter chips. They are the same kind of statement as the pills beside them — which page, then which players, which span, which position, which columns of them — and on the one page whose tab row would otherwise say almost nothing (Roster · Research and then a bare line) they are what the row is *for*. Left below the box they read as a second control area stacked under the first: two bands of chrome with a hairline between them and nothing on screen to say why they were two. Now there is one top section, sharing the ombré, the hairline and the gutters, and the page below starts at the count line, which is the table's caption rather than a control and stays where it was.

**Both wrappers are `display: contents`, and that is the whole layout rule.** `.view-bar-tabs` is already the app's answer to more groups than fit on a line — every group in it is atomic, and the wrap fits as many whole ones per line as the width allows, breaking *between* two rather than inside one. For the include buttons, the window tabs, the position row and the tools to take part in that they have to be items of *that* flex container, which is what `contents` buys; a box of their own would move as one block, so the set would either sit beside the Roster/Research pills or drop wholesale to the next line with nothing in between. With it, a 1920 window finishes the tab row with the entire control set on **two lines** where the page once opened on three, 1200 the same, and 390 is three rows of dropdowns and icons. The three include buttons cost that top width a line: measured on the same page before and after, the bar went 1→2 rows at 1920 (chrome 115→161px) and stayed as it was at 1200 (2), 900 (3), 640 (2) and 390 (3). **That line has since been won back** — dropping the `Qualified` button took the tools group from 581px to 468 and 1920 back to one row at 115px, which is where it was before the include buttons arrived. Almost all of that is the disclosure button the watchlist added rather than the buttons themselves — the tools group went 442→581px where the include group is only 32px wider than the scope switch it replaced — and a second line at 1920 is 46px against a control that answers "whose players am I looking at" without a dropdown. **390 was won back deliberately**: the group at its desktop padding measured 198px and pushed itself onto a line of its own, so under 640px it takes 8px of padding and a 6px gap and comes to 170, which is what lets it share the first row with the `Roster · Research` pills — 159 + the row's 12px gap + 170 is 341 against the 346 a 390px phone leaves inside the app's gutters. 375px and 320px are four rows either way at that width, and no width overflows (measured 320, 375, 390, 640, 900, 1200, 1920). **That arithmetic is a *two*-pill view switch**, which is what somebody with nothing on their roster gets; with the Roster and Feed pills showing it is three pills and **216.1px**, and 216.1 + 12 + 170 is 398 against 346 — so on the phone of anybody who actually uses the app the group had been on a line of its own all along, and the row this width class was tuned to save was already lost. Getting it back is what the **icon tier** below 480 is for: the group is 116 there, 216.1 + 12 + 116 is 344.1, and 390 goes four rows to three (see the include buttons' own entry under **research**). The gap becomes the row's own 10/12px rather than the bar's old 8, which is right: it is that row now. The panels and the chips row take `flex: 1 1 100%` — the "its own line" `.date-control` uses one level up — since a filter builder has no business sharing a line with the button that opened it. Note the selector for that has to be `.research-chrome > .research-panel` and not `.view-bar-tabs > .research-panel`: `display: contents` hoists the *boxes* into the flex container and leaves the DOM tree alone, so a child combinator still names the parent the element really has. Written the other way it matches nothing and the panel takes its content width — a 168px search field wedged onto the end of the tab row.

**App renders the box and `ResearchTable` portals its bar into it.** The controls could have been lifted to App instead, and that is the wrong half to move: every one of them is phrased in the board's *column vocabulary* — the picker lists it, the filter builder's select is built from it, the chips read their labels out of it — and all of that is the table's own, so lifting the bar would split one file in two and thread a dozen values back down to the rows. A portal moves the DOM and nothing else; the one price is that the host has to exist before it can be filled, which is why App keeps the element in state (`researchChrome`) rather than a ref and the bar is undrawn for the single commit before the callback fires — before paint, so nothing flickers. The box is rendered only on the research view, so no other page carries an empty row of it.

**It carries a tone of its own** — a vertical ombré from `--panel` down to `--bg-2`, the same two steps the cards are built from, under a soft accent glow at the top right echoing the one the page itself carries. The first version painted the body's *exact* gradient, pinned the same way (`background-attachment: fixed`), so the box lined up with the pixel behind it and was invisible until something scrolled under it. That was the wrong instinct for a bar that is always there: a strip which only appears once you have scrolled reads as an artefact of scrolling, where this is a permanent part of the app and should say so at rest. Both layers are the element's own rather than the viewport's, so the ombré runs across the bar's own height and reads the same whether it is 65px or the 300px of wrapped tabs a 320px screen produces. Deliberately shallow: measured off the rendered page it runs **#15233d → #111a2e** against a page of **#0d1425 → #0b1220** — about two steps of the app's own palette, enough to separate it and not enough to read as a panel sitting on one. A hairline and a shadow much softer than `--shadow` finish the edge; the shadow is what says the page passes *under* the bar rather than stopping at it.

Two places it stands down. **A short window opts out of sticky altogether** (`@media (max-height: 560px)`): a phone held sideways is about 390px tall against a chrome that reaches 303 at its widest wrap, and chrome that eats most of the screen is not worth the trade — below that height it scrolls away as it always did. Height rather than width, since height is what's scarce. And **`.app.summary-mode` / `.research-mode` opt out explicitly** rather than relying on sticky being a no-op in a column that doesn't scroll — which it isn't, quite: `overflow: hidden` makes that column a scrollport of its own, and a sticky box in one is held against its **padding** box, which undoes the negative top margin and leaves a 14px strip of page above the bar that no other view shows. Static there puts it back in normal flow, where the margin does what it was written to do. The error banner is deliberately outside the box, for the reason it is outside `.app.edit-mode`'s hide list: a failed report is news about the page rather than a control over it, and it has no business holding a permanent row against the top of the window.

**Everything that scrolls itself to the top now clears the bar** — `--chrome-h`, the bar's measured height, published on the document root by `hooks.ts::useStickyChromeOffset` and added to `--scroll-offset`, which every `scroll-margin-top` in the stylesheet reads. Pinning the chrome moved the top of the *window* out from under the top of the page, and the whole app scrolls to the window: `useScrollIntoViewOnExpand`'s `block: 'start'` on the collapsibles that are left — the game blocks on the two unrendered cards, everything else in the feed and every inning of an outing having become popups, which move nothing — and a clip when it plays. (A third was `App.tsx::scrollToPlayer`, the jump a name in the summary table used to make to a card on the grouped feed; that name opens the player page now, which *covers* the bar rather than clearing it, so the arithmetic has no caller left and is gone.) All of them went on landing their target 12px from the top of the viewport, which is now 115px (or 159, or 303) *behind* the bar — so opening a card scrolled it under the one thing that was pinned to stay visible.

It has to be **measured rather than declared**, because there is no one number: the bar wraps to two and three rows as the window narrows, and it stands down entirely on the summary and research views and under `max-height: 560px`, where the offset must go back to the bare 12px. So the hook reads the element's own height, and **reads `position` off the computed style to decide whether it counts** — the same answer the CSS gives, rather than a second copy in JS of the three rules that decide it. Three things drive a re-measure and each catches what the others can't: a `ResizeObserver` for the wraps, a layout effect on **every** render for a view swap that makes the bar static, and a **`resize` listener** for the one case neither sees — a *shorter* window unpins the bar without changing its height by a pixel or re-rendering anything, so a phone turned sideways would go on clearing 159px of bar that is no longer there. Layout effects rather than plain ones, so the property is set before the browser paints and before the scroll a click has just triggered reads it. The hook still hands its height back as a ref and nothing reads it any more — `scrollToPlayer` was the one caller that did its own arithmetic rather than going through `scroll-margin-top`, and it went with the jump. Checked in a browser at 900 and 390 wide and on a short window: a card, a game block, an at-bat and a feed item all land exactly 12px below the bar, and 12px from the top of the page wherever the bar isn't pinned.

**Each page keeps its own place, and going back to it lands where you left.** The Games view and the Feed were two readings of the same days over one window scroller, so the offset used to carry straight across a tab switch: leaving Games 800px down opened the Feed 800px into a stream of somebody else's at-bats, and coming back clamped to wherever the shorter page ended. (Those two are now one page and a toggle, so that exact pair can no longer collide — but Feed and Research still can, and the kind tabs still swap one page for another.) That is as old as the tabs and **only became reachable when the chrome was pinned** — they used to be at the top of the page, so getting to one meant scrolling back up first and a reset came free with having to go there. `App.tsx` now keeps a scroll offset per page, restored in a layout effect when the page changes. Keyed by view **and kind**, since the kind tabs swap the whole list for a different set of players — Feed/Batters and Feed/Pitchers are two pages by the same test — and **not** by the date range, which changes the numbers in the rows rather than which rows they are, so the row being read is still on screen. A page not yet visited opens at the top. The summary and research boards scroll an inner element rather than the window (`.summary-scroll`, `.research-scroll`, both fixed-height columns), so the memory reads and writes through whichever of the two is on screen; the listener is a single **capture-phase** one on the document, since a scroll event doesn't bubble and those inner scrollers would otherwise be unheard. The research board narrows that: the memory answers *which page* you are on, and the board answers *which table* — changing its population or its order scrolls it back to the top rather than restoring anything (see **research** below). App holds one more thing on the same terms and for the same reason — how far the feed's Recent section has been paged out (`feedShown`, below) — since an offset is only ever restorable into a page long enough to hold it.

**Nothing in this app bounces.** There is one value — **`none`** — on every one of the twelve `overscroll-behavior` declarations in the stylesheet, and the only thing still decided per element is *which axes* it is declared on, which turns on whether that box genuinely scrolls in the axis. The three wide tables — `.summary-scroll`, `.research-scroll` and `.glog-scroll` — take it on **both**, since each scrolls in both: reaching the last row or the last column used to hand the gesture straight on to whatever was behind, which for the game log is the player page's own scroller (a flick down a log carried on into the overlay it sits inside) and for the boards is the page, or on a phone the browser's rubber-band and pull-to-refresh. The overlays take it on both as well — `.details-view`, which the how-to and the league pages ride on, `.reel-view`, and the Columns dialog's own body (`.research-columns-body`) — alongside the pinned body `useLockBodyScroll` gives them, which is the other half of that behaviour and is untouched. The strips that scroll **sideways only** declare the inline axis alone, and deliberately: the details tabs, the research board's position row and window tabs, the tutorial's jump strip and the ESPN page's URL example. A scroll container blocks chaining on an axis it cannot itself scroll, so naming the block axis on a row of pills would swallow a vertical swipe that belongs to the page under it — which is why `overscroll-behavior-x` is the right half there and the wrong half on a table. And the **document itself** takes `none` on `html`, which is the same rule one level up: see the two paragraphs below.

**`none` rather than `contain` everywhere, and on an iPhone that is the whole of the difference.** `contain` stops the *chaining* and explicitly keeps the element's **own** overscroll affordance, which on iOS is the elastic bounce — so the page behind held still while the last row still slid up off the pane and sprang back, and the far-right column still pulled away from the edge. Read off a phone that is a table that has carried on scrolling past its own end, which is exactly the report. `none` keeps the chaining fix and drops the bounce, so the gesture is consumed where it lands. Nothing is lost anywhere with a pointer, there being no bounce to suppress.

**The overlays were the last things on `contain` and are not any more.** The argument for keeping them was that `.details-view` and `.reel-view` are *pages* rather than panes, and a page that refuses to give at its own end is a different bargain from a table that does. It reads well and it lost to the report: the bounce is unwanted on a player page for the same reason it is unwanted on a board, and a reader who has just been given a table that stops where its rows do does not then want the overlay above it springing back off the bottom of a percentile card. So the split is gone and there is one answer for the whole app, which is also one fewer rule to remember when a scroller is added.

**And the document mustn't bounce either — on every view, not on two of them.** iOS hands the drag to the page whenever the pane can't scroll in the axis it was dragged in — a six-row summary table on a tall phone, a board narrow enough to fit, any sideways swipe on either — and in `.app.summary-mode` / `.research-mode` the page is a `100dvh` column with `overflow: hidden`, so what moves is the whole app rubber-banding under the finger. That case is what the rule was written for, and it used to be **scoped to it**: `html:has(.app.summary-mode), html:has(.app.research-mode)`, on the reasoning that the feed is an ordinary scrolling page with a pull-to-refresh worth keeping. What that left is the report — the *page* bounces on the two views where it can actually scroll, which is most of the app's reading. So the scoping goes and **`html` takes `overscroll-behavior: none` unconditionally**, up beside `body` at the top of the file where a rule about the document belongs; `:has()` is no longer needed for it, and the summary and research case is covered by the same one line it used to need its own for.

**The cost is real and is one thing: pull-to-refresh on the feed.** It is worth paying twice over. The gesture only ever reloaded the tab, where the app's own re-read is the header's **Refresh** button, which is on every view and does more than a reload would — it re-reads every source the page in front of you is drawn from, and nothing else. And the bounce it buys back is the very thing being complained about. Declared on `html` alone rather than on `html, body`: the root's value is propagated to the viewport, `body`'s only when the root's is `auto` in both axes, and the body is not a scroll container here — so a second declaration would say nothing.

**Measured in a browser** (900×800, against the local server): `html` computes **`none / none`** on summary, games, feed, research, the how-to page, the player page and the league page alike, where it was `auto / auto` on games and feed before. `.summary-scroll`, `.research-scroll`, `.glog-scroll`, `.details-view` and `.reel-view` are all `none / none`; the five sideways strips — `.details-tabs`, `.research-positions`, `.research-window-row`, `.tut-nav`, `.espn-code` — are `none / auto`, the block axis still untouched. Everything still scrolls where it should: the summary pane to its 121px maximum, the research pane to 342 down and 250 across, the games page and the feed page to 600 (documents 1,725 and 4,146 tall), the player overlay to 500 of 1,104, and the how-to page to 700. No horizontal overflow of the page body on any view.

**`touch-action` takes the same discipline, and one element got it wrong.** The property says which gestures the *browser* may keep, so claiming more of it than an element reads is how a page stops scrolling: `.roll-chart` — the rolling-xwOBA chart on the player page — carried `touch-action: none`, while its handler reads `e.clientX` and nothing else (which plate appearance the finger is over). It had never wanted the vertical axis, and the vertical axis is the one the page is read with. On a phone that chart is a 332×138 band across the middle of the overlay, so a thumb landing anywhere on it could not scroll the player page at all — **"sometimes the scroll gets stuck", exactly**. Measured at 390×500 with a synthetic drag: starting on the chart moved the overlay 0px where starting 60px above it moved it the full 110px of its range; with `touch-action: pan-y` both move it 110, and a horizontal drag still tracks the crosshair (checked with a finger and with a pointer). It is the same mistake `.order-row` already documents — `touch-action: none` there made the whole page unscrollable in edit mode — and the two answers are the same shape: scope the property to the part of the gesture the element actually consumes, the grip in one case and the inline axis in the other. There are **two** declarations of `touch-action` in the stylesheet, and the count has been back and forth. It was three for a while, the third on the Columns dialog's `.research-order-grip`, and that one is now the sharpest reading of the rule there is — because the way it was settled was to **delete it** rather than to scope it better: see **the columns can be rearranged** below. The rule the count expresses is unchanged and is the thing to keep: the default is to let the browser scroll, and anything that departs from it owes a reason, in the smallest scope that answers it.

**What the third one was for, and what it could not answer.** The two that remain are the two shapes of the answer — the grip (an element the page does not scroll from) and the inline axis (an axis the page is not read with) — and the Columns dialog's reorder chips look like the first case, are not, and turn out not to be the second either. They looked like the first because of a browser behaviour worth naming once and remembering: **Chrome performs touch adjustment**, snapping a touch that lands near a small target onto it, so a `touch-action: none` region is effectively wider than the element that declares it by roughly the radius of the finger. Measured on the Columns dialog at 390px, a 9×13px grip swallowed a band from 10px left of it to past the chip's right edge — the scroll it forbade was forbidden over half the chip, and over the first six chips **40 of 85 sample flicks moved the dialog 0px**. Enlarging the grip makes it worse rather than better, the band growing outward from whatever box it is given. So the property went to `pan-y`, handing the block axis back to the scroller, and **that fixed the scrolling and broke the reordering**: those chips *wrap*, so the commonest reorder on a phone is a downward drag, and the axis rule threw exactly those away — measured, **10 of 30** drags landed where they were dragged. There was no third scope to try. What answered it was changing the *gesture*: on touch the drag is gone and a press picks a column up, which no scroller competes for, so the declaration went with it. The lesson the three of them make together is that `touch-action` can only arbitrate between two gestures that differ in **place or axis**, and when they differ in neither the gesture is what has to move. Where they share the axis — the edit screen, whose rows are dragged down a page that scrolls down — the element is the separator, and `.order-grip` keeps `none` for exactly that reason.

**Measured on the edit screen, so the cost of keeping `none` there is on the record**: the same snap widens `.order-grip`'s 27×58 box to a band running x=268–310 of a 346px row — **42px, 12% of it**, about 10px of which is the name column to its left. That is the price of a same-axis drag and it is paid deliberately: the grip is a target a finger can actually aim at, the other 88% of the row scrolls, and there is no axis to scope to. (Checked at 390×380, in the running app: a flick from the name scrolls the page 128px and reorders nothing; the same flick from the grip scrolls 0 and starts a drag.)

### A card doesn't highlight when you scroll past it

**`:hover` is the third property on that same list, and it had been left to itself everywhere but one table.** The report was plain — *the cards should not highlight when scrolling, e.g. on the feed Upcoming games* — and the cause is the one fact about hover that a desktop never has to think about: **a touch device has no pointer to move away**, so the state sticks to whatever was last touched until something else is. A finger dragged down the feed leaves the last card it crossed tinted; a card tapped to open a dialog is still tinted when the dialog is dismissed. Either way the app then declines to act on what looks exactly like a selection, and that is the confusing part rather than the tint itself: the mark says *this one*, and nothing about it is true.

**Reproduced before the change, on the live feed at 390×844 with touch emulation.** Tap an Upcoming row, close the split it opens, and the row's computed `border-color` is **`rgb(56, 189, 248)`** — the accent — against a resting **`rgb(38, 54, 92)`**, with `matches(':hover')` still true. The same tap on an at-bat card leaves its background at **`rgba(56, 189, 248, 0.06)`** against a resting `rgba(0, 0, 0, 0)`, on a pitcher's outing bar the accent border again, and on a faced-batter card inside an inning dialog the same 6% wash.

**So every hover on a pressable surface is scoped to `@media (hover: hover)`**, which is the rule `.glog-table tbody tr` has carried since the Game Log's rows became presses and is argued there (see **The Game Log's rows open the game** in the player-page notes — *with a pointer the tint is the truth, and on touch the press itself is the affordance*). What this is, is that one sentence applied to the rest of the app. The canonical comment sits at the top of `styles.css` beside `html { overscroll-behavior: none }`, where a rule about the whole document belongs, and each of the ten sites carries a one-line back-reference rather than a copy of the argument.

**What is scoped is a *surface*, not a control**, and the line is drawn on the size of the thing and on whether a finger crosses it without meaning to.

- **Scoped (10 rules).** The feed's at-bat rows (`.pa-summary-row`), its outing and Overview game cards (`.feed-item-toggle:not(.static)`, `.pday-game:not(.static)`), the Upcoming row (`.upcoming-head:not(.static)`), a pitcher's inning bars and faced-batter cards (`.inning-head`, `.faced-summary`), the card section bars (`.game-sub-bar`, and its `.static` counterpart which cancels it, moved inside the same condition so a rule and its reset cannot disagree about when they apply), the unrendered card's header (`.player-head-toggle`), a search result row (`.adder-option`), and the Columns dialog's reorder chips (`.research-order-chip.target`, and the grip that lights with them). Every one is a full-width row or card in a list that scrolls — or, for the chips, paints something a real selection state also paints.
- **Left alone.** Buttons, tabs, pills, icon squares, headshot circles and text links — some fifty rules. Their hover is a small affordance on a target you deliberately aimed at, they mostly live in the pinned chrome rather than in anything you scroll through, and a stuck tint on a 36px square reads as nothing at all where a stuck band across a card reads as a choice. The headshot circles (`.sum-photo-wrap`, `.feed-photo-link`, `.player-photo-link`) are the closest call and stay: they are 40–42px, and their hover recolours a border the live-role ring already paints through, which is a distinction a reader has to be looking for rather than one that arrives uninvited. The date picker's calendar cells stay too — a grid of aimed targets inside a popover, with `.in-range` and `.edge` painting selection far louder than a `--border` wash, and nothing that scrolls across them.

**The chips are the one small target that takes the rule, and for *what* they paint rather than how big they are.** On touch the reorder gesture there is a press and a press, so a chip is left hovered by every step of it; and what `.target:hover` draws on a drop target — full opacity and a solid accent border — is very nearly what `.picked` draws on the chip actually being held. Scroll the picker mid-reorder and the last chip the finger crossed says it is the one being moved, while the real one says so too.

**The two roster tables carry no row hover at all, which is worth recording rather than leaving to be rediscovered.** Both were swept and there is nothing there: `.summary-table tbody tr` and `.research-table tbody tr` measure the same background hovered and unhovered (`rgba(0, 0, 0, 0)` at 1200 on both), their only hover rules being the headshot's border and the name link's colour. So the widest, most-scrolled tables in the app were never part of this.

**Measured after, on the same surfaces at 390×844 with touch emulation.** Every one returns to its resting colour **while `matches(':hover')` is still true**, which is the evidence that wanted having: the element is still hovered and the *rule* is what stopped applying. Upcoming `rgb(56, 189, 248)` → **`rgb(38, 54, 92)`**, the at-bat row `rgba(56, 189, 248, 0.06)` → **`rgba(0, 0, 0, 0)`**, the outing bar's border → **`rgb(38, 54, 92)`**, the faced-batter card → **`rgba(0, 0, 0, 0)`**, and a `.target` chip **0 of 27 lit** where the grip reads `rgb(92, 111, 151)` (`--faint`) rather than the accent. Under that emulation `matchMedia('(hover: hover)')` is **false** and `(pointer: coarse)` **true**, which is what the query is reading.

**And with a mouse at 1200×900 every one of them still lights**, driven by dispatching `mouseMoved` onto each: Upcoming `rgb(38, 54, 92)` → `rgb(56, 189, 248)`, the at-bat row `rgba(0, 0, 0, 0)` → `rgba(56, 189, 248, 0.06)`, the outing bar to the accent, `.inning-head` `rgb(11, 18, 32)` → `rgba(56, 189, 248, 0.06)`, `.faced-summary` the same, `.adder-option` `rgba(0, 0, 0, 0)` → `rgb(22, 33, 58)`, and a `.target` chip opacity `0.7` → `1` with its border to solid accent and its grip to `rgb(56, 189, 248)`. Scoping, not deleting: a pointer user loses nothing.

**`:active` and `:focus-visible` are untouched everywhere**, which is the half most easily broken by a sweep like this — a momentary press state and a keyboard ring are wanted on every device, and neither can stick. The diff adds exactly one line naming either, and it is the sentence you are reading; checked on touch, `.pa-summary-row:focus-visible` still draws its `2px solid rgb(56, 189, 248)` outline with the media query evaluating false.

**Audited out of the CSSOM rather than by reading the file**, since a rule that merely *sits near* a media query is not inside one: all ten selectors plus the game log's original resolve `inside (hover: hover) === true`, at 390 and at 1200 alike, with nothing missing. **Bundle: JS unchanged at 454.23 KB** (134.39 gzipped) and CSS **104.65 → 104.87** (18.69 → 18.73) — 0.22KB raw and 0.04KB over the wire, nearly all of it the paragraph at the top of the file. No horizontal overflow of the page body on the roster, feed (both kinds), research or player page at either width.

**Restoring is one write, before the page is painted.** A layout effect on the commit that swapped the page sets the offset once, so the first frame of the new page is drawn where the reader left it — and it is *exact*, because the page being written to is the page the number was measured against.

That last clause is the whole of it, and it is what used to be untrue. The offset was applied to a page still assembling itself, so the write **clamped**, and the number then had to be *held* against a growing document — re-applied every frame with `overflow-anchor: none` switched on underneath it, until the height had been quiet for `PAGE_SCROLL_QUIET` (250ms) or `PAGE_SCROLL_SETTLE` (900ms) had passed. Every symptom of "it flashes and it doesn't land in the same place" came out of that one shape, and each was measured on the same roster and the same 1200×900 window (feed 4,529px settled, target 2,000):

- **The flash is the clamped first paint.** The returning feed was 2,639px, so `scrollTo(0, 2000)` landed at **1,739** — the bottom of a page that had not finished being itself — and the page was painted there for three frames before the clips landed and the hold caught up.
- **The jump is the frame in between.** On the frame the content arrived, the offset read **2,873** against a target of 2,000 — 873px past it — and was pulled back the next frame. Reproducible to the pixel on every run.
- **The inconsistency is the 900ms deadline.** With clip lookups answering in 50ms the hold wins and the landing is right; with them answering in 1.5s — a phone on a real network, or a cold `/api/video` — the hold gives up wherever the page has got to. Measured with the lookups delayed 1,500ms: a target of 2,000 **lands at 2,873**, one of 1,500 at **2,634**, and one of 3,200 at **2,873** — out by +873, +1,134 and −327 on three consecutive tests of the same page, with nothing on screen to say so.

**What removed all of it is not a better hold but the growth.** A clip's URL is resolved lazily, one request per play, and `InlineVideoClip` renders nothing at all until its own answers — so the height of the feed is a function of how many of its lookups have come back, and a remount (which is every tab switch) started them over. Now the answers are remembered for the life of the tab (`clipUrls` in `PlateAppearanceCard`, hits and misses alike), so a returning feed renders its clips in its **first commit**: sampled every frame across the swap, the page has one height, **4,529 from the first frame on**, where before it was 2,639 growing to 4,529. It also makes the returning page a faithful replica of the one that was left — someone who leaves mid-load comes back to the same partly-loaded page at the same instant of its history, rather than to a shorter one that grows differently under a stale number.

**What a size cache cannot answer is a scrollport whose own box isn't final**, and that was a second, quieter bug of its own. Both boards are fixed-height columns and each settles a frame or two after mount: `.research-scroll` is **26px shorter** then than it ends up. The inner branch was a single assignment with nothing to catch it, so leaving the board at 31,736 came back to 31,710 — and stayed there, permanently 26px off, on every visit (176 → 150 on a short board). So a clamped write is **repeated when the scrollport reports a new size**, driven by a `ResizeObserver` on the pane and its content (or on `document.documentElement` for the window), and it ends on a **condition** rather than a clock: the offset has been reached, or somebody else has taken the scroll. That second test needs no list of events to watch, because it is the same test either way — the reader scrolling and the research board resetting itself to the top both move the offset off the value we wrote, and either is a reason to stop.

**`overflow-anchor` is untouched now.** The hold used to switch the browser's scroll anchoring off across the whole document for up to 900ms after every tab switch — a defence against a stale pixel target being dragged about by content arriving above the viewport, which is exactly what the 2,873 excursion was. With nothing arriving, anchoring is on our side: it is the thing that keeps a reader's place when the 20s live poll inserts an at-bat above them, and it has no business being off.

**`restoring` is now one frame long, and it has to be at least that.** It stops the scroll event our own write raises from being recorded as the reader's own, which would overwrite a remembered 2,000 with whatever the write was clamped to; a scroll event is dispatched in the rendering step *ahead* of that frame's animation callbacks, so a flag cleared in a `requestAnimationFrame` covers it exactly and no longer. The old flag was held for the length of the hold, which is also the window in which the reader might have scrolled themselves. Checked: leave the research pane at 176, cross to the feed and back twice — 176 both times, the clamped 150 never having been written down.

**Measured after, on the same page, the same roster and the same two clip speeds** — first paint and landing, at 1200×900 and at 390×844: feed ↔ research at 2,000, feed ↔ roster at 1,500, the kind tabs at 1,200, a deep offset at 3,200 and the research pane at 176 all land **exactly**, with the first painted frame already on the offset (no clamp, no excursion past it), warm and with the clip lookups delayed 1,500ms alike. The deliberate behaviours were driven in a browser and are unchanged: a page not yet visited opens at the top (0); a reader's own scroll 30ms into a restore wins (they asked for 300 and got 300); the research board still returns to the top when its population changes (4,000 → 0 on the `SS` pill) and still doesn't on a refresh or a tick of the column picker (4,000 → 4,000 on both); and the player page still resets to the top on a tab switch (500 → 0).

**Nothing places a scroll by hand any more**, and the browser's own restore on a reload owns the first pass. Two things used to. `openPlayerDay` scrolled to the player's card and set a `scrollPlaced` flag so the page memory wouldn't restore the Games view's offset out from under it; and before that a `backScroll` restored the origin view's offset itself, once in a `requestAnimationFrame`, before the feed had finished growing — landing 1900px past where it was aiming, which is what retired it in favour of the memory. Both are gone with the jump they served: a name opens the player page, which is an overlay over wherever you were rather than a place on another page, so there is nothing to place and nothing to come back from.

**Narrow screens** get one vertical step, `--stack-gap` (12px), applied by a `@media (max-width: 640px)` block **at the end of `styles.css`** — every row stacks on a phone and each was spaced by whichever rule owned it (the header's gap, the view bar's, the tab group's, the card list's), reading down the page as 20, 14, 10, 14, 18. The block is last on purpose: a media query adds no specificity, so it has to come after the base rules it overrides. The **settings popover** (the gear beside the title) has its own narrow-screen rule, next to the component rather than in that block: it normally hangs off the gear, which on a phone sits some 200px into a 390px header — less room to its right than the menu needs, and Sign out carries the signed-in email, so a real address ran off the screen. Below 640px `.settings-menu` and `.fantasy-menu` go `position: static` and `.brand` becomes the containing block, so either menu drops from the same edge but opens at the header's left. Its `max-width` (`min(320px, calc(100vw - 44px))`, the 44px being the app's side padding) is what keeps the email ellipsizing inside the menu instead of deciding how wide it is.

**Kind tabs.** All three — the summary table, the feed and the reorder screen — show **one kind at a time**, driven by a single shared `.kind-switch` row (Batters / Pitchers, the word alone — each carried a count until it was cut) — `App.tsx` builds it once as `kindTabs` and hands each view `kindCards` (the reports for `shownKind`). It renders in the **view bar**, in `.view-bar-tabs` with every other group (so the bar's `space-between` doesn't push them apart), second in the row and wrapping with the rest as the width dictates. Persisted as `kind=pitcher` (batter is the default and omitted). **Loading: one spinning baseball, three sizes, and a rule about when to show it.** The app had six loading affordances and now has one. What was there: a 13px CSS ring (`.spinner`) drawn as a border with one accent edge, forced to 15px in the fantasy popover and left at 13 in the header button; an `.refreshing` pill saying `Updating…`; a bare `.loading` line saying `Loading events…`; five `.details-status` lines on the player page each saying `Loading …` about a different tab; the research board's count line saying `Loading league leaderboard…`; an `.empty-state` box titled `Reading your league…`; `Loading highlights… 4/18` in the reel; `Reading…`, `Checking…` and `Working…` inside three buttons; `Loading players…` as a disabled input's placeholder; and — the first thing anybody sees — **nothing at all** while `/config.json` came back, `main.tsx` rendering `null` over a blank window. Plus one dead rule, `.content-layout.is-loading .player-list`, whose class nothing had set in either workspace.

They are all one thing now: **`components/Loading.tsx`** (the glyph and the two wrappers), the `.ball-spin` family in the stylesheet, and `hooks.ts::useDelayedFlag`.

**The glyph is `BaseballMark`'s ball, turned into a ball that turns.** That is the point of starting from it rather than drawing a spinner: the accent baseball is already the app's mark for "this player is on your roster", on the fantasy button and on every rostered row of the research board, so a wait drawn as the same object reads as the app thinking rather than as a widget from somewhere else. The rim is drawn once and **never transformed** — a sphere's silhouette doesn't move — and the seams sweep across it, clipped to the rim. That clip is the whole difference between a ball rotating about an axis and a disc turning in the page plane.

**The seams are placed by the projection a point on a sphere actually has**, which is what makes it read as a sphere rather than as a wheel: horizontal position `9·sin θ` and horizontal foreshortening `scaleX(cos θ)`, so a seam is widest face-on, narrows to a hairline at the limb, and **bunches** there. That last part is the tell — marks on a *disc* bunch at the centre and vanish, marks on a *sphere* pile up at the edges — and it is the reason a plain `rotateY` on the seam group was rejected: a flat plane turned about its own Y axis is the shadow of a spinning disc, exactly the wrong physics. Opacity carries a seam round the back, fading out over the last eighth before the limb where it is already a hairline, so nothing pops.

**Four seams, 90° apart, bulging alternately left and right**, and each of those three numbers was forced. A seam is on the visible half for 180° of the turn, so the spacing has to be *under* 180 or the ball goes momentarily blank — with two seams (the first attempt, being what the mark itself draws) a quarter of every revolution had nothing on it at all, checked against the phase arithmetic. At 90° there are always two seams on the front, or one face-on with two edge-on. And the pose at every odd eighth of the turn is **exactly `BaseballMark`**: one seam right of centre bulging left, one left of centre bulging right, which is why the spinner passes through the app's own mark four times a revolution and the two read as one object.

It is **nine keyframes of `transform` and `opacity` on four paths, and no JavaScript at all**. Nothing lays out, nothing paints outside the element's own box, and the compositor has four small layers to move. Two details make one keyframe set correct at every size: `transform-box: view-box` is what makes `translateX(9px)` mean nine *viewBox units* rather than nine CSS pixels, and `transform-origin: 12px 12px` is the ball's own centre. The stroke is deliberately left to scale with the transform — a seam thinning as it foreshortens is the same fact stated twice — where `vector-effect: non-scaling-stroke` would have pinned the ink to device pixels and made the 14px ball heavy and the 44px one spindly.

**Reduced motion holds the turn at the `BaseballMark` pose and pulses instead.** `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` stops the seam animation, gives the two visible seams the `±6.36 / scaleX(0.707)` pose by hand (the other two are hidden behind them, so nothing has to be rewritten per seam), and runs a 1.8s opacity pulse between 0.45 and 1 on the whole ball. A *still* ball is a logo and says nothing about whether anything is still happening; a fade is not the kind of motion that preference is asking to be spared. Measured with the media feature actually forced on: `animationName` is `none` on all four seams and `ball-pulse` on the ball, and the four transforms are byte-identical a second apart.

**Three sizes, one per situation** — declared by the class, since the box is the *slot's* business and the glyph's job is to be the same object at any of them.

- **`sm` (14px)** — inline. Inside a control that was pressed (the header's Refresh, the fantasy popover's Refresh from ESPN, the ESPN page's Refresh and Connect, the sign-in form's submit), or leading a one-line status that sits **beside** data rather than instead of it (the `Updating` badge, the research board's count line, the roster search's field). It is one number where the old ring was two — 13px in the header and forced to 15 in the popover — and the two slots that want their own size still say so in one place each: `.refresh-btn .ball-spin` is **16px**, because the arrow it replaces is a three-quarter circle of radius 8 in a 17px box against the ball's radius 9 in its own, so 16 is where the two silhouettes match and the swap reads as the arrow closing into the ball it was drawing; `.fantasy-refresh > svg` is 15px, the rule that already held the arrow's slot, and it needed no widening because the ball *is* an `svg`.
- **`md` (28px)** — a block wait, for a pane with genuinely nothing in it yet: the report's first read, the research board's first read, each lazily fetched tab of the player page, the highlight reel, and the ESPN league read the board's last two include buttons depend on.
- **`lg` (44px)** — the boot splash, which is the one wait that owns the whole window and has nothing behind it to protect.

**What each wait says is the point, and it is the app's own empty-state rule applied to waits.** An emptied board names the button that emptied it; a wait names what is being read. So `Reading the league leaderboard`, `Reading your roster's games`, `Reading the percentile card` / `the season line` / `the game log` / `the season arsenal` / `the season's plate appearances`, `Reading your ESPN league`, `Finding the clips — 4 of 18 plays`, `Reading the season roster`, `Starting up`, `Signing you in`. Five of those were the word "Loading" and a noun, on five panes, which told a reader nothing they could not already see.

**The trailing ellipsis goes wherever a ball sits beside the words.** `Loading…`, `Reading…`, `Checking…` all spelled out "still going" in punctuation, which is precisely what the turning ball is for; and under reduced motion the pulse says the same thing. So the label is a plain verb phrase and the mark carries the tense.

**When a wait is shown at all** is the rule that had to survive intact, because the app has spent a long time getting it right in four separate places: the report's quiet path, the research rows written on success alone with `loading` gated on the cache being empty, the ownership map left in place while the next read is in flight, and the fantasy roster the same. Stated once:

1. **Never over data.** If the pane has rows, the read is quiet and no ball appears in it. The only marks a re-read may leave are *inside the control that started it* and the in-place `Updating` badge for a foreground read the reader didn't press anything for. Checked in a browser: with `/api/report` held open and a preset changed from Today to Yesterday, the badge appears and all 21 feed items stay exactly where they are.
2. **A block wait only when there is nothing to show yet**, and only after **`WAIT_DELAY` (250ms)**. That floor has guarded the report since it was written and is now `hooks.ts::useDelayedFlag`, which every block wait in the app takes — the five player-page tabs above all, where a warm percentile card comes back in tens of milliseconds and a wait that appears and vanishes inside a tenth of a second reads as the page breaking rather than as an answer. The *content* stays gated on the real flag, not the delayed one, or a fast tab would show a blank pane instead of a wait.
3. **A press-triggered mark holds `MIN_SPIN` (450ms)**, unchanged and now stated as the other end of the same argument: `MIN_SPIN` is a floor on how long a mark stays up once a press put it there, `WAIT_DELAY` is a delay before a mark goes up at all for the waits nobody pressed. The two are deliberately different numbers because they answer different questions. The ESPN page's Refresh inherits the floor through `onRefresh`, which *is* `refreshFantasy`; the sign-in submit and the Connect league button take neither, a Cognito or ESPN round trip having no fast answer to leave a press without a trace.

**Where it sits and what colour it is.** The ball is `currentColor` and `.ball-spin` sets `color: var(--accent)`, so there is one loading colour in the app and it is the accent — including inside the header's Refresh while that button is `:disabled` and otherwise `--faint`, since it is disabled *because* it is working. The rim carries `stroke-opacity: 0.5`: at full accent the silhouette is the loudest thing in the mark and the seams — the only part that moves — read as detail inside it, which is backwards for something whose whole job is to look like it is turning. In the **pinned chrome** it is a 16px glyph in a 36px square that does not move, so nothing in the header shifts; **inside an overlay** it is the same `md` block the page uses, centred in the player page's own reading column, and the details view's five tabs get it in the box `.details-status` already owned — that class is folded into `.loading-block`'s selector list rather than restyled to match it, the rule this stylesheet applies everywhere, and what is left under its own name is only what a *failed* tab adds (`.details-error`'s colour).

**`.refreshing` keeps its name and its pill and is now `.loading-line` plus that pill**, so the badge and the board's bare caption are one object cut two ways. `LoadingLine` takes an `announce` flag that is off inside a control: the button already says `aria-busy`, and a live region nested in a button is the same news announced twice.

**The boot splash is new rather than restyled.** `main.tsx` rendered `null` while `/config.json` was in flight, which on a cold load is a blank window with nothing in it — the one wait in the app with no indicator at all, and the first thing anybody sees. It now shows the same `Splash` card the gate's own boot state uses (exported for it), so the two steps of starting up read as one screen with the ball at 44px under the product's name.

**The date controls are behind `.date-toggle` at every width** — not just on a phone. They are 576px of pills and picker — measured against the 1136px content width the app used to cap itself at, where they were easily the widest thing in the chrome — and they are set once and then read for the rest of a session, which is the shape of a thing that belongs behind a button. On a phone the button that opens them is the same calendar reduced to its icon, with the range on a bubble — see the starters toggle's note below, which the two share. The range picker deliberately doesn't, its own popover needing the row to stay put. Both are hidden on the research board, which has nothing dated to act on, and with the rest of the chrome on the edit screen — by being inside `.view-bar`, which that mode hides, rather than by being named in its list.

**The button is on the roster row, not in the header, and it says which days it holds.** Those were two controls until now: a square calendar icon up in the header and a round `dateBadge` chip down in the view bar — the page stating the span in one place and letting you change it in another, and a chip that could only be read sitting an inch from a button that only opened. One control does both, and the label *is* the state: **`activePreset ?? numericRange(start, end)`**, so it reads `Today` while a preset is active — that is what was picked, and it survives the date rolling over — and `8/1 – 8/9` once a range is picked by hand. **Numeric on purpose**: it sits in a wrapping row of tab groups, so every character it spends is one that can push the group after it onto the next line, and the month name buys nothing the number doesn't (the year buys less still — the app shows one season and says so nowhere else on the page). It keeps its label at every width, alone among the chrome's buttons in doing so: the label is the only thing on the page saying which days every number on it is drawn from, and the icon alone would leave that unsaid.

It is on the roster row for the reason the roster tabs are: the dates qualify exactly those three views and nothing on the research board, so a header slot made it chrome belonging to the whole app when it belongs to one page of it. **Last in the row**, after the tab groups — it answers "which days", the question that comes after "which page", "which kind" and "which reading of them".

The empty-roster state gained a **`.empty-search`** button beside its `.empty-help` one (`.empty-actions` is the row, `.empty-state` being centred block flow where two inline buttons would be separated by one collapsed space). The search being an icon is a small target to hand someone with nothing on screen, so the one page a new user is guaranteed to land on opens it for them.

**The edit screen takes the page.** With it up, `.app.edit-mode` hides the gear, the fantasy button, the date control and its toggle, the view bar and the whole header cluster — everything the app draws above the page. Hidden in CSS rather than dropped from the JSX because it is one statement of one idea where the equivalent is five conditionals scattered through the render; it has to sit **after `.view-bar`** in the file to win, which is why it's down beside `.header-tools` rather than up with the header rules. The **error banner is deliberately not in that list** — a mode is no reason to stop saying the report failed. The rule used to name the magnifier *inside* the cluster rather than the cluster itself, and had to: the cluster held the Edit button, which had to survive as Done. With Edit in the gear's menu, `.header-tools` goes wholesale — and so does `.fantasy-menu`, the one control that was still standing here, on a screen where all it could do is swap the very list being edited.

**Done therefore lives on the edit screen's own head**, beside the title and the kind tabs, and had to move there the moment Edit became a menu entry: the header button was both the way in and the way out, and a menu entry can only ever be the way in — the gear is hidden in this mode, so a Done left inside it would be behind a button that isn't there. A mode with no visible way out is a trap, which is the one rule this screen has always had. It keeps `.edit-order-btn` and its `active` fill, so the control that leaves the mode looks like the one that used to enter it, and it keeps its label at every width now that no header line budget is arguing with it. `.edit-page-head` is `justify-content: flex-end` with `margin-right: auto` on the title, which is what holds the switch and Done together at the right end — with three items `space-between` would strand the switch in the middle of the row — and at 390px the row wraps with Done dropping to a line of its own under the switch, still right-aligned, which is a full 36px target rather than a squeezed one.

The screen carries its own `.edit-page-title`, since the chrome that would otherwise say where you are is gone, and **`kindTabs` comes with it** onto `.edit-page-head`: the view bar is hidden here and it is the only way to the other kind's order, so hiding the chrome would otherwise mean a roster whose pitchers can no longer be reordered. `.edit-page` is folded into `.order-editor`'s 620px centred column so the heading and the switch line up with the rows under them. Every control in those bars shares `--control-h` (36px) so the row reads as one set, while keeping its own text size. The header's **date presets are the same segmented control**: `.date-presets`/`.date-preset` are folded into the `.view-switch`/`.view-tab` selector lists rather than restyled to resemble them, so the selected preset highlights identically by construction (watch the `@media (max-width: 640px)` hide rule, which needs `.date-row .date-presets` to outrank the shared `display: inline-flex` that comes later in the file). Corner radius is one token too — `--control-radius` (12px) on every button, tab shell, form field and date control app-wide, `--control-radius-inner` (9px) on a tab inside a switch so its corners sit concentric with the shell. Fully-round pills are now only *labels* (status chips, outcome badges, the lineup pip): if it can be clicked it takes the control radius, which is what keeps the chrome in the same family as the cards' softer boxes. The search field is **not** inflated to 16px for iOS's sake: an inline script in `index.html` adds `maximum-scale=1` to the viewport meta **on iOS only**, which suppresses Safari's zoom-in-on-focus (it never zooms back out) without touching pinch-zoom, which Safari has allowed regardless since iOS 10. Other browsers do honour `maximum-scale` and would lose zoom entirely, hence the UA check. If a future iOS ignores it, the fallback is the blunt one: 16px on `.adder-input` under `@media (pointer: coarse)`.

**Injured players.** A player on the IL plays no games, so over a range that starts after he went down every column of his is a dash. **Both the summary table and the feed drop him only when the settings menu's "Hide injured players" is on** (`hideil=1` in the URL, off by default, and **saved per user** — see **Watchlist, users and auth**) — one toggle deciding it for both, so the setting means what it says wherever you are. The summary table used to drop him **outright**, whatever the toggle said, on the reasoning that its rows are nothing *but* those columns; that was wrong twice over. A row of dashes against a name is itself the answer to "is he playing?", which is a question you ask a roster table — and a table that silently omits a player on your roster is worse than a sparse one, since nothing on screen says he was left out, where a dash says so on his own row. It also made the toggle a lie in the one place it was most visible: turning "Hide injured players" *off* changed nothing on the summary, and its own tooltip had to admit the exception. A URL saying nothing about the toggle is *unspecified* rather than off, which is what lets the saved value fill it in; since `hideil=1` is the only thing the param can say, a shared link can only ever turn the filter **on**. A `hideInjuredTouched` ref keeps a preference that lands late from undoing a toggle the user has just worked. The **feed is left alone either way**: it's a chronological record of things that happened, and an at-bat a player took before the IL stint is still an at-bat he took. `App.tsx` filters into `shownReports` **ahead of the kind split**, so the tab counts equal the list under them; the reorder screen is deliberately upstream of it (`editPlayers`, off raw `reports`), since dropping an injured player from the roster is what that screen is for. Note the badge on a summary row is `rosterStatusBadge`'s, which is MLB's roster status and so has nothing to say about a player who is **day-to-day or out** — those come from ESPN and are covered under **ESPN fantasy league** above. The test is `isInjured` (`lib.ts`), which is `rosterStatusBadge`'s `il` tone **plus a rehab assignment** — code `RA` isn't a D-code so the badge files it under "other", but a rehabbing player is on the IL and just as absent from a box score. Deliberately narrower than "not on the active roster": a suspension or an option to the minors is a few days and can end with a recall mid-day, an IL stint is weeks. When the filter empties the active view an `.empty-state` says so — otherwise the summary is a header over a Total row of zeros, and the feed an expanse of nothing with no hint that a setting caused it. It is **one message for every view** now, naming the toggle and where to find it, where the summary once needed its own wording to explain a rule no setting controlled. It is one message for every *cause*: the roster row's `Starters` filter (below) can empty any of these three views too, and it gets its own wording rather than sharing this one — a reader sent to the gear to undo a button in the tab row would be looking in the wrong place. The two can't both fire, since this one requires the kind's list to be empty before the other is applied. **Mute clip audio** is the menu's other saved toggle, and the only preference in the app that isn't in the URL at all. Hide-injured is there (`hideil=1`) because it changes *which players a view is reporting on*, and a link that says so is saying something about the data; muting is about this person and this room, so a shared link has no business carrying it — which also means there is no param for a recipient's own saved value to have to defend itself against, and the load logic is a plain "apply unless the user has already worked the toggle" (`muteAudioTouched`, the same ref pattern hide-injured uses). Stored as the absence of a key when off, like the other two.

It reaches the clips through **`MutedContext`** (`hooks.ts`) rather than a prop: every clip in the app plays through `ClipVideo`, so one provider around `App`'s tree and one `useMuted()` in that component covers the cards, the feed, the player page and the highlight reel — and anything added later — where a prop would thread a boolean through six components with no other interest in it.

**The preference is each clip's starting value, not a rule over it.** `ClipVideo` holds its own `muted` state seeded from the context, and an `audioTouched` ref that is set the moment the viewer works *this* clip's audio — with the button below or with the browser's own controls, which `onVolumeChange` mirrors into the same state so the two can't disagree. From then on the preference leaves that clip alone. Without it, unmuting one clip to hear a call was undone by any later re-read of the preference: checked before the fix — unmute clip 0, toggle the setting off and on, and all three clips came back muted. `GameReel` keys `ClipVideo` on `playId`, so the reel advancing remounts it and the next clip starts from the preference again, which is what "per clip" should mean. `muted` is set **both as an attribute and imperatively in a layout effect**: React treats it as a property and has historically missed it on the initial mount, which on an `autoPlay` clip is the one failure that matters, since the setting exists precisely so a clip cannot start making noise on its own — and a layout effect runs before paint, so the element is never briefly live.

**`.clip-audio`** is the per-clip mute, over the **top right** of the frame, and `ClipVideo` gained a `.clip` wrapper purely to be its positioning context (a plain block, so the three players keep the widths their own classes give them). It exists because the browser's own mute is only reachable with a pointer: on a touch device the control bar is hidden for the *whole* time a clip plays (see the `ClipVideo` note above), which with clips muted by default would leave no way to hear one at all. **Top** because the native bar runs along the bottom; **right** because the top *left* is where the broadcast's own score bug sits — on all three clips checked — so the chip was landing on the count and the inning. The trade is that some feeds put a network bug in the right corner (two of those three), which the chip now clips a corner of; that one is smaller and comes and goes, where the score bug is there for the whole clip. It carries its own dark chip rather than `--panel`, sitting as it does on video that is a different colour every frame.

**A `.clip-seek` pair sat beside it — back 5s / forward 5s — and is gone.** The argument for it was the mute's own one step further on: on a touch device, while a clip is playing, there is **no scrub bar at all** (`ClipVideo` drops `controls` for the whole of it), so the only way to see a play again was to let it end and start it over. What that bought was not worth what it cost the frame. A baseball clip runs six to twelve seconds, so "let it end and press play" *is* the re-watch, five seconds either way being most of the highlight anyway; and on the one device the pair was for it put three 36px chips across the top of a 332px picture — 84px of it for the two skips, measured — to save a viewer a few seconds of a clip they were already watching. With them go `SEEK_STEP`, `SEEK_END_GUARD`, `seek()`, the `SeekIcon`, the `.clip-seeks` layer and `keepPress`; **`--clip-w` stays**, having become the mute's own anchor the moment it moved right (below).

**A skip forward stops a quarter second short of the end rather than on it.** Landing on the duration fires `ended` there and then, and in the highlight reel `onEnded` advances to the next at-bat — so a forward skip would read as "next highlight", with no way back to the play being watched. Short of it, the clip plays out its own last frames and ends because it ran out, which is the one ending everything downstream is already written for; paused, it holds on the final moment, which is what "skip to the end" means anyway. **A skip back on a clip that has already ended resumes it** — it is sitting on its last frame with the controls back up, and skipping back there plainly means "show me that again" — while a clip the viewer *paused* stays paused, since they stopped it on purpose and a seek is not a request to start it. Backwards clamps at 0. Nothing is held in state: the buttons read and write the element, so the reel remounting `ClipVideo` per `playId` carries nothing across.

**The right edge has to be measured, where the left one didn't** — which is why the mute's move cost it the simple `right: 8px` it looks like it should have. The `.clip` wrapper is a plain block and two of the three players leave slack inside it: the feed's frame is capped at 640px in a column that reaches 786 at 1200px wide (measured), and the games-view clip sizes to the clip's own dimensions — so a button at the wrapper's right edge would sometimes sit *beside* the picture rather than on it. That was the whole reason the mute lived on the shared left edge, and the reason the observer written for the skip pair outlived it: `ClipVideo` publishes the video's rendered width on the wrapper as **`--clip-w`** from a `ResizeObserver`, the way `useStickyChromeOffset` publishes `--chrome-h` — there is no one number to declare, the clip's dimensions arriving with its metadata and the frame reflowing with the column. The chip reads it as `left: var(--clip-w, 100%)` with `translate: -100% 0` and an 8px negative margin, so its far edge lands on the video's and the arithmetic holds at **both** sizes it takes without either being restated. It positions **itself**, where the pair needed a `pointer-events: none` layer spanning the frame to be laid out as a group — one button needs no group, and no invisible layer across the top of the picture means there is nothing left to swallow the tap-to-pause. Checked in the feed at 390 and 1200 and on an intrinsically-sized clip: the chip lands 8px inside the **video's** right edge in every one, including the 1200px feed case where the wrapper is 786 and the picture 640 — and 36px square under `(hover: none)`, verified with the hover media feature actually forced off.

**They don't disturb the pause-on-tap**, and the reason is structural: they are siblings of the `<video>`, not children of it, so a press on one never reaches the element's own handlers — the shadow-DOM confusion that forced that gesture onto `pointerdown` cannot arise here. What they do stop, on `pointerdown` as well as on the click, is the press travelling *up* into the cards and feed items the clip sits in, which are full of toggles. Checked both ways round: with a pointer, +5 from a paused clip leaves it paused and moves it 5s; with a coarse pointer and `hover: none`, a tap on the button while the clip is playing seeks and leaves it playing, and a tap on the bare frame beside it still pauses.

The settings toggle is `.settings-toggle`, **folded into `.sim-toggle`'s selector lists** rather than restyled to match, so the menu's two rows are one control by construction; only the active colour differs (the simulate overlay is a temporary lie about the data, red; this is an ordinary preference, accent). **Edit players** and **How to use** take `.help-btn` and read last, below the toggles, in that order: each opens a screen rather than flipping a setting, so they are the menu's way *out* of it.

**Edit players is a menu entry rather than a header button**, which is where it lived until now — a pencil in `.header-tools`, labelled from 641px up. It is a control reached once in a session, by a user who has decided to reorder or drop somebody, and it was holding ~84px of the one row in the app whose budget is measured in whether a 418px search field fits (see above: the header now goes to one line 40px narrower than it did, *and* has gained the refresh button). It renders on the same terms it always did — more than one player, and a watchlist of ours rather than ESPN's, since ESPN owns that list and a screen offering to rearrange it would be offering something it can't do — and it does the same two things on the press: **the reorder screen only exists on the Roster page**, so pressing it from anywhere else switches there rather than flipping a mode with nothing on screen to show for it — it moved off the Games view with everything else when that view went, and Roster is where it belonged anyway, being the page about the roster as a *list* rather than as a stream. One thing had to move with it: `.app.summary-mode` makes the page a fixed-height flex column with `overflow: hidden`, which is right for a table that scrolls itself and wrong for a long list of draggable rows, so the class is now `view === 'summary' && !editMode`, and the search bar is closed on the way in so it isn't restored on the way out of a mode it was never visible in. It also **closes the popover**, which it must: edit mode hides the gear, and a menu left open would be floating over a screen that has hidden everything it belongs to. Edit mode is still transient — deliberately not in the URL — and still clears on a view switch. The fantasy popover beside it is built from exactly these parts — `.settings-popover` for the box, `.settings-toggle` for "Use my fantasy team", `.help-btn` for "League settings" — by folding the class onto the same element rather than restyling a second menu to match, so the two are one menu cut two ways.

**Every search box in the app matches on a folded name** (`lib.ts::searchFold`), because a fifth of this league's surnames carry an accent and nobody types one. `garcia` finds García, `pena` finds Peña, `jimenez` finds both Jiménezes — and the reverse holds, which is the half that is easy to forget and the half a phone keyboard's autocorrect produces: typing `Suárez` used to find the two men MLB spells with the accent and hide the three it spells without, so *both* spellings returned a partial list and neither reader could tell. There are two of these boxes and they now answer identically: the header's roster search (`PlayerAdder`, over the ~1,400-row season roster, in the header form and the phone search bar alike) and the research board's own search over the whole league (`ResearchTable`, name **and** club abbreviation).

**The fold is: NFKD, drop the combining marks, lowercase, remove every non-alphanumeric.** That last step is what lets one `includes` answer the whole punctuation question at once rather than a rule per mark — `Crow-Armstrong`, `O'Neill`, `J.T. Realmuto` and `Ronald Acuña Jr.` become `crowarmstrong`, `oneill`, `jtrealmuto` and `ronaldacunajr`, so `crow armstrong` / `crow-armstrong` / `crowarmstrong` are one query, `o neill` / `o'neill` / `oneill` are one, `j.t.` and `jt` are one, and `acuna jr` and `acuña jr.` are one. Note the **suffix is kept** where the server's `normalizeName` strips it: that one is matching two whole names against each other and wants `Luis Garcia Jr.` and `Luis García` to meet, where this one is matching a *fragment into* a name — strip `jr` here and a search for it is a search for nothing.

**A fold rather than `localeCompare`.** `Intl.Collator` at `sensitivity: 'base'` is the other way to call `garcia` and `García` equal and was the alternative considered; it compares whole strings, and there is no collator-aware `includes`, so substring matching with one means sliding a window along every row by hand. The fold gets the same answer out of the engine's own `includes` — and, the half that decides it, **it can be computed once per row and held** where a collator runs per row per keystroke. Both surfaces do exactly that: the roster search folds each player once per roster (a `useMemo` over `players`, which arrives once), the board folds each row into a `Map` keyed on the row object (so the include buttons rebuilding `boardRows` through a `filter` re-fold nothing), and each keystroke folds only the query. Measured on the real board: **0.215ms** to fold all 703 rows once, **0.059ms** for a keystroke against the held haystacks against 0.126ms folding per row.

**Name and club are joined by a space, and that space is load-bearing** — a folded query can never contain one, so it is a separator no typed string can straddle and `garcialad` cannot match a García of the Dodgers. The roster search joins the player's name to his `savantName` the same way, which is what makes **surname-first work**: `ramirez jose` and `peña jeremy` now find their man, where the old code matched `savantName` only if you typed its comma. Its inner spaces *are* collapsed, deliberately and by the same rule as `Crow-Armstrong` — which is why `sd` picks up "Elias Díaz" and "Beavers, Dylan". That is a false positive at two characters in a menu that shows eight rows of eleven hundred anyway, and it is the price of the seam that spans a name.

**Checked against the real season roster and both real boards**, old matcher against new, over 26 queries: **nothing the old search found is lost** — the new set is a superset every time — and the rows both find come back in the same order, so nothing about the boards' sorting or the menu's eight moved. What it gains: `garcia` 7 → 10 roster rows and 4 → 6 board rows, `José` 12 → 22, `acuna` 0 → 2, `jimenez` 0 → 2, `pena` 0 → 1, `crow armstrong` 0 → 1. Driven in a browser on both surfaces for García, Ramírez, Peña, Suárez, Acuña, Jiménez, Sánchez and Rodríguez, each typed both ways round; `rodriguez` and `Rodríguez` both now return the same six pitchers, mixed spellings together, which neither one reached before.

**One definition per workspace, mirrored by hand.** The workspaces cannot import from each other — the same constraint that has `client/src/types.ts` mirroring `server/src/types.ts` — so the accent-stripping step is `stripAccents` in `client/src/lib.ts` and `stripAccents` in `server/src/names.ts`, and nothing else in either workspace spells out the NFKD-and-strip again: `savantPlayerUrl` had its own copy and now stands on the client's, and `espn.ts::normalizeName` (the ESPN name join, which needs a *different* normalisation — suffixes gone, punctuation to spaces) now stands on the server's. `searchFold` itself is client-only, there being no typed-input search on the server. `names.ts` is where the server's goes because it is the module named for exactly this, and it keeps the count at one per workspace rather than the four a third copy would have made.
